# The Meridian Combine

*Background. None of this is in the game's own fiction, and none of it appears on the
weapon cards in the loadout screen.*

## The firm

The Meridian Combine was founded during the trading years as a joint Boscali and Primevan
industrial venture. The revolution and the war left it belonging to neither. It kept its
plant on neutral ground, kept its order books open, and now sells to both of its former
parents.

A meridian is a line on a map that belongs to nobody. A company owned jointly by two
governments cannot be named after either of them, so the founders picked a surveyor's
abstraction, agreeable precisely because it meant nothing. It was a diplomatic
convenience at the time and it turned out to be worth more than the plant.

Its position has never been dressed up as principle: it sells to buyers, not to causes.
Both customers find that easier to tolerate than the alternative, which is the other side
having the hardware and them not.

## How its weapons are named

Combine stores carry a class prefix and a number, the way most ordnance does. Not every
round earns a name, and the ones that do are named by the works rather than by the buyer.

The first generation was given animals, picked for temperament rather than appearance.
The generation that followed was given blades instead, and nobody at the works will say
which director decided it or why. The house explanation is that an animal suits a weapon
you release and forget about, and a blade suits one you have to aim.

It began as a filing habit and stuck because crews used the names anyway. A round with a
name gets asked for by name.

## The catalogue

**AGM-84 Warhawk.** Heavy, optically guided, and the weapon the Combine puts in front of
a buyer first. A television seeker is locked on before release, a datalink refreshes the
aimpoint in flight, and the warhead is built for hardened structures rather than vehicles.
Released and forgotten about, which is where the name comes from.

**AGM-57L Bulldog.** Laser guided, with a blast warhead sized for soft and lightly
armoured targets. It carries no seeker that can find anything by itself and rides a
designation held by somebody else, so it goes exactly where it is pointed and nowhere
else. The Combine has never pretended otherwise in its own literature.

**AGM-33L Hornet.** The light laser round, sized for helicopters and light attack
aircraft. It exists because a customer wanted a guided answer to armour at a price that
fire and forget could not reach, and was willing to hold the laser to get it. Small, and
rarely carried one at a time.

### Air to air

**AAM-41 Gram.** The long reach, and the round the Combine is proudest of. It carries two
booster settings and chooses between them at launch: a hard, short burn for a target
close and ahead, where what matters is the energy to run something fast down, and a
longer, gentler one for anything far out or wide off the nose, building speed slowly and
then holding it on a sustainer that runs for over a minute. The hundred kilometre figure
in the sales literature is the range from which a target cannot simply turn and run, not
the range at which the motor gives up.

**AAM-63 Falchion.** The medium round, and the one that sells. It stays quiet for most of
the flight and only lights its own radar near the end, which lets the aircraft that fired
it turn away early instead of holding the target through to impact. Buyers who have flown
both say the Gram wins the argument and the Falchion wins the war.

**IRM-L7.** Heat seeking, and much longer legged than a heat seeker has any business
being. It emits nothing at all on the way in, so the aircraft it is aimed at gets no
warning from its receiver and no notice until the round is close. It is also stubborn
about flares once it has settled on something. It has no name, which crews have taken to
mean the works could not think of a blade unpleasant enough.

**SRM-8 Kukri.** Close in, and heavier than the rounds it shares a rail with. It reaches
a little further than a dogfighting missile usually does and gives up some of its turn to
do it, which is a trade the Combine defends in print and pilots argue about in the bar.

### Anti radiation

**ARAD-72.** It homes on a radar that is transmitting, and it is built for the long shot
rather than the close one: a hard boost off the rail and then a sustainer, so it still
has speed left when it arrives. Against an operator disciplined enough to shut down it is
an expensive way to make a point. Against one who is not, it is the whole argument.

### Bombs and stand off

**GBO-900.** A heavy glide bomb with a camera in the nose and no motor at all. Dropped
from height and well outside a defended area, then steered the rest of the way. The
Combine markets it as the cheapest thing it sells that a defended target cannot ignore.

**AGM-92.** The GBO-900 with a motor slung underneath it, burning long and slow rather
than hard, which is what turns a glide into a cruise. Flown onto the target exactly the
same way. It is the most expensive round in the catalogue and the works has never
apologised for that.

**GBP-500 Bodkin.** Hardened, and built to be through the concrete before it does
anything. The camera locks the target before release and it needs nothing from the
aircraft afterwards. Named for the arrowhead that was made narrow on purpose, for going
through the one thing everybody else's arrows stopped at.

**AGR-40 Hairpin.** Guided rockets, in a four round pod or a twelve round pod, fitting
wherever the unguided pods already fit. The Combine's answer to a customer who wanted
precision without paying missile prices for it, and who was told, correctly, that he
would be trading warhead for the privilege.
